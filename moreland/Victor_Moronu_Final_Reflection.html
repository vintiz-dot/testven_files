Educational Technology Project Reflection

Victor Moronu
Master of Education in Educational Technology
Greenfield Preparatory Middle School

The culmination of the Educational Technology Capstone Project has been an incredibly validating and demanding experience. Designing a comprehensive, data-driven instructional coaching program for Greenfield Preparatory Middle School allowed me to synthesize theories of adult learning, instructional design models like ADDIE, and technology integration frameworks such as TPACK and SAMR. Presenting my findings to the cohort and receiving their feedback provided a critical lens through which to view my work, forcing me to defend my instructional decisions and iterate on my initial assumptions. This reflection addresses the feedback received, the lessons learned from both the project and my peers, and how my fundamental understanding of educational technology has evolved throughout this Master’s program.

Cohort Feedback and Peer Presentations

Presenting my capstone pitch to the cohort was an illuminating experience. My presentation focused on a three-layer coaching program designed to combat device distractions in our 1:1 Chromebook environment while scaling differentiated instruction. The feedback I received from my peers was predominantly encouraging but constructively critical. One peer pointed out that while my ADDIE-based coaching cycles for individual teachers (like Ms. Vectore and Mr. Luma) were deeply structured, I had not fully addressed the logistical barriers of securing substitute coverage for peer observations during the "Improve" phase of Knight’s Coaching Cycle. This feedback was invaluable; it highlighted a blind spot in my administrative planning.

Watching my peers’ presentations was equally educational. I was particularly struck by a cohort member who tackled augmented reality (AR) for differentiated literacy instruction. Their project used the SAMR model not just as an evaluation tool, but as a student-facing rubric, empowering students to self-assess the depth of their digital creations. Seeing how my peers applied the same foundational theories—whether it was the Algo-Heuristic Theory for computational thinking or Merrill’s First Principles for gamified learning—demonstrated the incredible versatility of our coursework. It reinforced that while our contexts vary wildly, the pedagogical frameworks that underpin effective technology integration remain universally applicable.

What Would I Do Differently?

If I were to begin this project again, my primary adjustment would be in the initial Needs Analysis phase. While my conditional-logic survey successfully captured quantitative data regarding teacher confidence and administrative priorities, it relied too heavily on self-reported data. Teachers overwhelmingly flagged device distractions (78%) and requested PD on classroom management (61%). However, self-reported data often treats the symptom rather than the disease.

Next time, I would supplement the survey with a week of passive classroom observations to gather objective, qualitative baseline data. I would want to see how the distractions are occurring before designing the intervention. Are students off-task because the digital curriculum is not engaging, or simply because the physical layout of the classroom prevents the teacher from monitoring screens? By incorporating a formal observation rubric into the initial gap analysis, the subsequent professional development sessions would be even more targeted and actionable.

The Most Important Lesson Learned

The single most important lesson I learned from this capstone experience is that technology integration is fundamentally an exercise in change management, not IT deployment.

Early in the program, I assumed that if you provided teachers with a powerful tool (like GoGuardian or an interactive Desmos activity) and showed them how to click the right buttons, instructional transformation would naturally follow. The project taught me that the tool itself is the least complex variable in the equation. The real challenge is navigating adult learning theory—overcoming teacher anxiety, respecting their limited time, and shifting their pedagogical beliefs. Using the GROW model and Knight's Coaching Cycle taught me that a coach's role is not to dictate tech usage, but to serve as a collaborative partner who helps teachers solve their own self-identified pedagogical problems using digital tools. Pedagogy must always drive the technology, never the reverse.

Application in My Career

Moving forward in my career as an educator and aspiring EdTech leader, I will leverage the structured frameworks I mastered during this project. Specifically, the ADDIE model will become my standard operating procedure for designing any professional development or curriculum rollout. Rather than creating "sit-and-get" workshops based on administrative whims, I now have the tools to conduct rigorous needs analyses, design targeted interventions, and evaluate their efficacy using Kirkpatrick's Four Levels of Evaluation.

Furthermore, the capstone experience has equipped me with the empirical research needed to advocate for job-embedded instructional coaching. When speaking to school boards or district leadership, I will no longer just advocate for buying new software; I will advocate for investing in the human capital required to integrate that software meaningfully. I can now back my proposals with data, cost-value analyses, and peer-reviewed literature proving that continuous coaching yields higher student achievement than isolated training events.

The Evolution of My Definition of Educational Technology

When I began this Master’s program, my personal definition of Educational Technology was highly instrumental. I viewed it as the hardware and software deployed in a classroom to make learning more efficient or engaging. I defined it by the things—the Chromebooks, the interactive flat panels, the learning management systems.

Today, my definition has fundamentally shifted. I now define Educational Technology not as a set of physical tools, but as a systematic process for solving educational problems and enhancing human capacity.

"Educational Technology is the strategic application of digital tools, grounded in learning theory, to remove barriers to understanding, differentiate instruction at scale, and empower students to become creators rather than mere consumers of information."

This shift from a tool-centric view to a process-centric view is a direct result of grappling with TPACK and the systems thinking required for the capstone. Technology is no longer the event; it is the invisible infrastructure that supports equitable, universally designed learning experiences. This program has transformed me from a "tech enthusiast" into an instructional designer, and I am deeply excited to bring this strategic vision to my campus and beyond.
