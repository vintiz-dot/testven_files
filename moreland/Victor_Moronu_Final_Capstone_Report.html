Section 1

Introduction

This report is the culminating submission of the TEACH-NOW / Moreland University Master of Education in Educational Technology Capstone Project. It documents the design, planning, and initial implementation of an EdTech Coaching Program at Greenfield Preparatory Middle School — a 1:1 Chromebook campus serving approximately 540 middle school students across Grades 6–8 with 18 Math and Science teachers and 4 administrators.

The capstone project was built across four sequential phases spanning the program's modules:

  1. Technology Infrastructure Survey (Module 40, Unit 4) — collecting live data on the school's current technology landscape, teacher confidence levels, and instructional challenges.
  2. Professional Development & Instructional Coaching Workshop Plans (Module 41, Unit 4) — designing four targeted PD sessions and two individual coaching recommendations grounded in survey findings.
  3. Learning Design Solution & Technology Implementation Plan (Module 42, Unit 4) — creating a three-layer coaching architecture (1:1 cycles, PLC data routines, design thinking sprints) with a 12-month implementation timeline and four capstone milestones.
  4. Implementation, Research, & Final Report (Module 43) — conducting statistical research and a literature review, pilot-testing the coaching cycle, collecting implementation artifacts, and producing this final report.

The core insight driving every phase is a single sentence from the survey data: teachers at Greenfield Prep already operate classroom technology — 13 of 18 use Chromebooks daily — but cannot reliably design with it. The solution is not a tool. It is a coaching program.

Contribution of Capstone Activities to the Final Submission

Each capstone activity was not a standalone assignment — it was a load-bearing phase in a single, continuous arc from diagnosis to implementation. Below I detail how each of the three early capstone activities contributed to this final project.

2.1 Technology Infrastructure Survey (Module 40)

The Technology Infrastructure Survey was the diagnostic foundation of the entire project. Without it, every subsequent decision — from the coaching model to the technology stack — would have been based on assumption rather than evidence. The survey was deployed as a conditional Google Form to 18 teachers and 4 administrators at Greenfield Prep between April 13–17, 2026.

What it contributed:

  • Baseline data that made the case for coaching: 78% of teachers named device distractions as their top challenge; 67% rated differentiation confidence at 3/5 or below; 44% had received no training on GoGuardian, a tool already licensed by the school.
  • The coaching demand signal: 67% of teachers said "Yes" to 1:1 coaching, and an additional 28% said "Maybe" — a 94% combined interest rate that validated the coaching model before it was designed.
  • Administrator priorities that shaped the evaluation metrics: all four administrators named "data-driven instruction," "purposeful device use," and "classroom management look-fors" as their top concerns. These became the program's measurable success metrics.
  • Named teacher coaching targets: administrators identified specific teachers and specific pedagogical goals, which directly informed the two coaching recommendations in Module 41 and the pilot cohort selection in Module 42.

Without the survey, the coaching program would have been a generic proposal. With it, the program is a data-driven response to a documented, school-specific gap.

2.2 Professional Development & Instructional Coaching Workshop Plans (Module 41)

The PD and Coaching plans translated the survey findings into actionable training designs. Four PD sessions were proposed, sequenced to hit the biggest survey pain first (device management) and ladder up to the more ambitious goals (differentiation, interactivity, data-driven instruction).

What it contributed:

  • PD Session 1 ("Pocket Check") — directly addressed the 78% distraction finding and the 44% who lacked GoGuardian training. This session became the template for the Q1 coaching focus area (routines) in the implementation plan.
  • PD Session 2 ("From Data to Groups") — addressed the admin priority of data-driven instruction. The GROW protocol used in this session was later adopted as the secondary coaching model for the 1:1 coaching cycle.
  • PD Session 3 ("Hands-On Science") — targeted the 56% who wanted interactive-lesson PD. The TPACK framework used here became a standard audit tool across all three coaching layers.
  • PD Session 4 ("Differentiation Without Drowning") — addressed the 67% confidence gap on differentiation. The four-tier scaffolding bank designed in this session informed the Q3 differentiation coaching focus.
  • Coaching Recommendations — the two individual coaching plans (Ms. Vectore for data-driven instruction, Mr. Luma for interactive lessons) established the blueprint for the 1:1 coaching cycle protocol, including the five-meeting arc structure that the full program adapted into its weekly Plan→Observe→Reflect→Apply cycle.

2.3 Learning Design Solution (Module 42)

The Learning Design was the architectural heart of the project — the document where survey data, PD plans, coaching models, and instructional design theory converged into a single, implementable system.

What it contributed:

  • The three-layer coaching architecture — weekly 1:1 coaching cycles, biweekly PLC data routines, and quarterly design thinking sprints — which is the defining innovation of the entire capstone project.
  • The 12-month implementation timeline with 12 named milestones, four of which (M1–M4) fall within the capstone implementation window.
  • The technology stack philosophy — fit-for-purpose over breadth — which ensured the program costs only ~$9,500/year (excluding FTE) because almost every tool is already licensed.
  • The evaluation framework with seven measurable success metrics tied directly to the survey baselines.
  • The pilot design — four self-selected teachers running two coaching cycles each in July 2026 — which became the action research vehicle for the implementation phase.
Each capstone activity functioned as a stage in a single design process: the survey diagnosed the problem, the PD plans proposed the treatment, and the learning design engineered the delivery system. Removing any one would have left the project incomplete.
Section 3

Phase I — Information Gathering

Target Audience

The program targets 18 Math and Science teachers (Grades 6–8) and 4 administrators at Greenfield Preparatory Middle School, a private, independent 1:1 Chromebook campus serving approximately 540 students. The student population includes approximately 28% multilingual learners (ML) and a significant inclusion population with IEP and 504 accommodations. The teachers range from first-year educators to 15+ year veterans, creating a wide spectrum of technology confidence and pedagogical experience.

Purpose and Goal

The program's purpose is to close the gap between technology access and purposeful technology integration. The goal, stated as a SMART objective: By May 2027, 16 of 18 teachers will design and deliver at least one data-driven instructional move per week using existing tool dashboards, and median teacher confidence on differentiation with technology will rise from 3/5 to 4/5.

Learning Levels

The program is designed to move teachers through Bloom's taxonomy from Understanding (current state: teachers understand how to operate tools) to Creating (target state: teachers design original lessons that leverage technology for student creation and differentiation). The SAMR model is used as the parallel framework: from Substitution/Augmentation (current median) to Modification (target median).

Research Reviewed

The learning design was informed by six theoretical frameworks: Costa & Garmston's Cognitive Coaching (1:1 cycle structure), Mishra & Koehler's TPACK (technology-pedagogy alignment), Puentedura's SAMR (walkthrough look-fors), Knowles' Andragogy (respect for teacher experience), Landa's Algo-Heuristic Theory (separating procedural routines from heuristic coaching), and the Stanford d.school's Design Thinking (quarterly sprint structure). These were selected for their direct applicability to adult professional learning in technology-rich environments.

Section 4

Phase II — Learning Design Solution

The Learning Design Solution is a one-year, three-layer coaching program. Each layer addresses a different aspect of the Phase I gap, runs on its own cadence, and feeds the others. The full design is detailed in the Learning Design Solution & Technology Implementation Plan.

LayerWhat It IsFrequencyPrimary Outcome
1. 1:1 Coaching Cycle Cognitive-Coaching-based Plan → Observe → Reflect → Apply cycle between the coach and one teacher around a teacher-chosen problem. Weekly, ~30 min One specific, applied instructional move per cycle.
2. PLC Data Routine Grade-team meeting structured around tool dashboards (IXL, Desmos, EdPuzzle) and SAMR-banded walkthrough look-fors. Biweekly, 45 min Shared instructional move from data; calibrated look-fors.
3. Design Thinking Sprint Compressed Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test cycle around a single high-leverage instructional problem. Quarterly, 90 min One student-creation lesson shipped & archived in showcase.

Why three layers instead of one: A coaching cycle alone produces individual gains but no schoolwide consistency. A PLC alone produces shared talk but rarely shared practice. A workshop alone produces an event with a half-life of about three days. Combining the three — with the same vocabulary (TPACK, SAMR, Cognitive Coaching, design thinking) running through all of them — produces individual change, team consistency, and schoolwide visibility.

Section 5

Phase III — Technology Implementation Plan

The implementation plan spans 12 months (August 2026 – May 2027) with 12 milestones. The capstone window covers Milestones M1–M4 (June–July 2026): securing admin approval, completing the tool audit, building the Looker Studio coaching dashboard, and running a pilot with four teachers.

Technology Stack

The program rests on a deliberately small stack. The principle is fit-for-purpose over breadth: nothing is included unless it directly serves a layer of the program. Key tools include Google Workspace for Education (coaching documentation), IXL/Desmos/EdPuzzle (data dashboards for PLC and coaching), GoGuardian (device management), and a Looker Studio coaching dashboard (the only net-new build).

Budget

Total program cost (excluding existing FTE): approximately $9,500 for the year. The dominant cost is people-time, which is already substantially in place. Specific line items: Newsela subscription ($2,400/yr), substitute coverage for release days ($5,400), prototype materials ($200/yr), showcase logistics ($300), and optional external coach visits ($1,200).

Section 7

Statistical Research & Data Review

The statistical research phase served two purposes: (1) to compare our survey data against published benchmarks and (2) to identify quantitative evidence that could inform modifications to the implementation plan.

Key Statistical Findings That Informed the Plan

Data PointSourceFindingModification to Plan
PD hours vs. integration quality JDET (n=73) Negative correlation: r = −0.276 (p<0.018). More PD hours ≠ better integration. Reinforced the decision to replace hour-based PD with coaching cycles. This statistic appears in the admin pitch as the strongest evidence that the current PD model is failing.
Active modeling correlation JDET (n=73) Positive correlation: r = 0.377 (p=0.001). Active modeling is the only PD variable that positively correlates with integration quality. Every coaching cycle now includes a "model-and-step-back" step where the coach demonstrates an instructional move before the teacher tries it.
Coaching impact on student outcomes Coaching & Technology Integration (n=132) Coached teachers (n=64) produced higher student performance and critical thinking behaviors than control group (n=68). Added student-work artifact collection to the coaching cycle protocol as a concrete measure of instructional impact.
Active learning engagement Engageli (2024) Active sessions yield 13× more learner talk time and 62.7% participation rate vs. 5% in lectures. Shifted PD session design from presentation-first to activity-first. The quarterly design sprint was redesigned to maximize hands-on time (70 of 90 minutes).
Digital storytelling achievement ERIC (n=856) Student-generated digital content: experimental group post-test mean 75.21 vs. control 54.97 (p=0.001). Validated the Q4 "student creation" coaching focus and the showcase as the culminating assessment.
Active learning and equity PNAS meta-analysis (1,294 sources) Active learning significantly reduces achievement gaps in STEM, with disproportionate benefit for underrepresented students. Strengthened the equity rationale for the differentiation strand (Q3) and informed the ML/IEP coaching focus.
The statistical research did not merely validate the plan — it changed it. The negative correlation between PD hours and integration quality was the single most influential finding: it converted the coaching program from a "nice to have" into the only evidence-based alternative to a demonstrably failing PD model.
Section 8 · Rubric Question: Literature Review Role

Literature Review & Modifications to the Implementation Plan

Fourteen articles were reviewed across the capstone implementation. Five were selected for deep analysis in the Action Research Report based on their direct relevance to the three-layer coaching model. The full literature review spreadsheet catalogs all 14 sources with key themes, purpose, relevant data, reliability assessment, and implementation relevance.

How the Literature Informed Modifications

1. Observation Protocol Adjustment (from Articles 1 & 2)

Both the Digital Promise study (50 schools) and the ERIC coaching cycle study emphasized that the "observe" step must be anthropological, not evaluative. Teachers who felt safe during observation were significantly more willing to experiment with unfamiliar tools. Modification: I added an explicit statement to the coaching protocol card: "The coach's job during observation is to notice, not to advise. Write what you see. Save what you think for the reflect step." This language was not in the original protocol and is a direct result of the literature review.

2. Dashboard Build Acceleration (from Article 5)

The PLoS ONE study on PLCs and digital innovation demonstrated that PLCs with shared data produce faster competence gains because they create social accountability. Modification: The dashboard (M3) must be functional before the first PLC meeting in August — not just "live" but populated with real data from the pilot. I moved the data-model build one week earlier than originally planned.

3. Design Competence Focus (from Article 4)

The Frontiers in Psychology systematic review (38 studies) found that teachers show adequate daily digital skills but particular weaknesses in digital content creation and pedagogical integration. This finding mirrors our survey data exactly. Modification: The Q3 (differentiation) and Q4 (creation) coaching foci were elevated in priority. The mid-year audit now includes an explicit "design competence" check alongside the SAMR walkthrough.

4. Peer Champion Scaling (from Article 1)

The Digital Promise Dynamic Learning Project found that coached teachers begin coaching their peers, creating a multiplier effect that scales impact without proportional increases in coaching staff. Modification: Two pilot slots are deliberately assigned to veteran teachers (Mr. El-Sayed, Ms. Mariscal) whose participation becomes the most credible recruiting story for the schoolwide launch. The program now includes a formal "peer coaching slot" where lead teachers run one PLC per quarter.

5. Pilot Documentation Upgrade (from Action Research)

The raw transcript from the LEGO prototype session demonstrated that audio transcription alone produces data that is too unstructured to analyze efficiently. Modification: For the remaining pilot cycles, I will use a structured observation template (printed, one-page, with pre-defined look-fors aligned to the SAMR bands) alongside audio recording. This produces both quantitative (look-for counts) and qualitative (coaching dialogue) data per session.

Literature review summary: The literature did not merely validate the coaching approach — it produced five concrete modifications to the implementation plan, each with a traceable connection to a specific research finding. The review functioned as a mid-course correction mechanism, which is exactly what action research is designed to do.

Learning Design Changes Going Forward

Based on my findings, peer and instructor feedback, and experience from this project, I would make the following practical changes to the learning design and technology implementation plan:

Changes Based on Findings
  • Observation protocol rewrite: Add explicit "notice, don't advise" language to every coaching card. The pilot session and the literature both confirmed that evaluative observation kills experimentation.
  • Dashboard build acceleration: Move the Looker Studio build one week earlier. PLCs without shared data default to opinion-based discussions, which the literature shows are less effective.
  • Structured observation templates: Replace pure audio transcription with a printed one-page look-for template aligned to SAMR bands. This produces analyzable quantitative data alongside qualitative coaching dialogue.
Changes Based on Peer/Instructor Feedback
  • Don't collapse the sprint into the PLC: A cohort member warned that merging the design sprint into regular PLCs would kill the "playful frame." I will maintain the sprint as a separate, protected 90-minute block.
  • Surface ML/IEP earlier: A cohort member noted that differentiation should not be "buried at Q3." I will add ML/IEP sub-bullets to the coaching focus in Cycles 1–3 (Q1) as a parallel awareness thread, even though the dedicated focus remains in Q3.
  • Asynchronous pre-work: Instructor feedback suggested that foundational tech-skills training (GoGuardian basics, dashboard navigation) should move to asynchronous video libraries, freeing synchronous coaching time for higher-order design work.
Changes Based on Experience
  • Pilot as co-design: Framing the pilot as "help me break this before we roll it out" rather than "try this program" produced dramatically higher engagement. I will use this framing for the schoolwide launch.
  • Photo + audio documentation: Adding photographic evidence alongside audio transcripts makes artifacts richer and more persuasive for stakeholders.
  • Custom survey over Google Forms: Building the survey as a web application gave more control over data structure and made the results page a shareable artifact for admin.
Changes for Year 2 Planning
  • Teacher-led PLCs: By Q3 of Year 1, lead teachers should be facilitating PLCs without the coach present. This is the sustainability model the Digital Promise research validated.
  • Student voice in the sprint: Year 2 design thinking sprints should include student co-designers. The empathize step currently uses teacher-reported student data; student presence would make it more authentic.
  • Cross-school coaching network: If Greenfield's model succeeds, the coaching dashboard and protocol can be adapted for other campuses in the network.

Next Steps & Recommendations

The following practical and actionable recommendations are directed to the leadership of Greenfield Preparatory Middle School and, where applicable, to the broader school network.

Immediate Next Steps (June–August 2026)

  1. Complete the Looker Studio dashboard build (M3) by July 3. The dashboard must be populated with real pilot data — not sample data — before the first PLC meeting in August. This is the highest-priority technical deliverable.
  2. Run the full pilot cohort (M4) through two complete coaching cycles each by July 31. The four pilot teachers (Ms. Ortiz, Mr. Patel, Ms. Mariscal, Mr. Guzman) should complete both cycles using the revised protocol with structured observation templates.
  3. Secure the Newsela subscription before August. The ML/IEP differentiation strand depends on it.
  4. Schedule the GoGuardian training during the August pre-service PD week, using the "Pocket Check" PD session design from Module 41.
  5. Publish the schoolwide launch plan based on the pilot retrospective data. This plan should include the coaching schedule, PLC restructuring timeline, and the first quarterly design sprint prompt.

Medium-Term Recommendations (2026–2027 School Year)

  1. Fund the EdTech Coach position as a dedicated FTE. The pilot data from July should be used to make the business case: coaching produces measurable changes that software purchases alone cannot. The statistical research (r = −0.276 for PD hours vs. integration) is the strongest evidence that the current model is failing.
  2. Restructure PLC meetings using the data routine protocol. This requires no additional budget — only a change in the meeting agenda. Every PLC should start with a dashboard on the wall, not a slide deck.
  3. Administer the re-survey in April 2027 using the same instrument deployed in April 2026. This provides the longitudinal data needed to measure the program's impact on teacher confidence, tool usage, and differentiation practice.
  4. Host the year-end showcase in May 2027. Each teacher presents one creation lesson from the year. The showcase doubles as the program's public evidence base and a recruiting event for Year 2.

Long-Term Recommendations (Beyond Year 1)

  1. Scale the coaching model to additional campuses using the Looker Studio dashboard, coaching protocol cards, and PLC data routine as a replicable kit. The coaching program was deliberately designed to be portable.
  2. Transition to teacher-led coaching by Year 2. Lead teachers who complete 12 coaching cycles should be equipped to run peer coaching slots, reducing dependence on a single coach.
  3. Integrate AI literacy into the coaching program. The literature review identified emerging AI integration as a gap that coaches will need to address. A Year 2 sprint prompt might be: "Redesign one assessment so AI helps students learn, not just answer."
  4. Connect coaching data to student outcome data. The current dashboard tracks teacher-level inputs (cycles completed, PLC participation). Year 2 should add student-level outputs (MAP/ERB score changes, student-work quality rubrics) to close the loop between coaching and learning.
The most important recommendation is the simplest: keep coaching. The research is unambiguous — sustained, job-embedded coaching outperforms every other PD format studied. The program does not need to be perfect in Year 1. It needs to be present, consistent, and responsive to data. Everything else is iteration.

Conclusion

Final Thoughts

This capstone project began with a simple observation: Greenfield Preparatory Middle School invested in technology, but not in the people who use it. The survey confirmed the observation with data. The PD plans proposed a response. The learning design engineered a system. And the implementation phase tested that system against reality.

The three most important lessons from this project are:

  1. Data changes conversations. Before the survey, the coaching program was an idea. After the survey, it was a response to documented teacher need. The difference between an idea and a mandate is evidence.
  2. Coaching is not training. The literature is unambiguous: more PD hours do not produce better integration. Active, job-embedded coaching does. The negative correlation (r = −0.276) between PD hours and integration quality is the most important number in this report.
  3. Plans are starting points, not endpoints. The implementation phase produced five concrete modifications to the original plan — each traceable to a specific research finding or pilot observation. A plan that does not change in contact with reality is a plan that is not listening.

The EdTech Coaching Program at Greenfield Prep is not finished. Milestones M2–M4 are in progress; the schoolwide rollout begins in August; the year-end showcase is nine months away. But the foundation — the survey data, the coaching architecture, the research base, the pilot protocol, and the administrative support — is in place. What remains is the work itself: one teacher, one cycle, one instructional move at a time.

"Technology will not replace great teachers, but technology in the hands of great teachers can be transformational." The purpose of this coaching program is to put technology not just in teachers' hands, but in their heads — where it becomes a design tool, not a delivery device.
